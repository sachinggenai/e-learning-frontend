### PAGE 2 — Why Every Employee is a Cybersecurity Defender
**Template:** Text Content
**Purpose:** Set the scene — explain why cybersecurity is everyone's responsibility and what learners will gain.

**Page content (enter as HTML in the editor):**

In today's connected workplace, cybersecurity is no longer solely the concern of the IT department. Every person who uses a computer, sends an email, or connects to the internet is both a potential target and a critical line of defence. Cyberattacks cost organisations worldwide hundreds of billions of pounds each year — and the vast majority of successful breaches begin with a single human action: clicking a malicious link, using a weak password, or inadvertently sharing sensitive information.

This course, **Cybersecurity Basics for Every Employee**, is designed for anyone who works with digital tools, regardless of their technical background. If you use email, browse the web, or access company systems, this course is for you.

**What you will be able to do by the end of this course:**

- Explain the most common types of cyber threat and how they work
- Identify phishing emails, suspicious links, and social engineering tactics
- Apply strong password practices and understand multi-factor authentication
- Know exactly what to do — and what not to do — if you suspect a security incident
- Handle sensitive data responsibly in your day-to-day work

**Why this matters:**

The consequences of a cyberattack extend far beyond the IT team. A data breach can expose customer records, damage your organisation's reputation, trigger significant regulatory fines under data protection law, and disrupt operations for days or weeks. In some sectors, a serious breach can threaten an organisation's licence to operate.

When an attack succeeds due to a human error, it is rarely because of carelessness or bad intent. Attackers are sophisticated. They craft messages that look exactly like communications from your bank, your CEO, or a trusted colleague. They exploit urgency, fear, and curiosity — deliberately, and with practised skill.

Understanding how they think is your best defence.

**How this course is structured:**

The course is divided into five modules covering the core concepts you need to stay safe online. You will explore real-world examples, interact with practical exercises, test your knowledge with formative quizzes, and complete a graded final assessment. Upon passing, you will receive a certificate of completion.

The course takes approximately 45–60 minutes. You can work at your own pace and return to any section at any time. No prior technical knowledge is required — just an open mind and a willingness to take your personal role in cybersecurity seriously.

Let's get started.

### PAGE 3 — The Four Types of Cyber Threat
**Template:** Tabs
**Purpose:** Introduce the four major threat categories with enough detail for learners to recognise each in the real world.

**Tab 1: Phishing**

Phishing is one of the most common and damaging forms of cyberattack. A cybercriminal sends an email, text message, or instant message that appears to come from a legitimate source — a bank, a government agency, a well-known retailer, or even a colleague. The message creates a sense of urgency, asking you to "verify your account", "reset your password", or "review an urgent document". When you click the link or open the attachment, you may be taken to a fake website designed to harvest your login credentials, or a file may silently install malicious software on your device.

Phishing attacks are highly effective because they exploit human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. Even experienced professionals can be fooled by a well-crafted phishing email. The best defence is to slow down, question unexpected requests, and verify the sender through a separate channel before taking any action.

---

**Tab 2: Malware**

Malware — short for malicious software — is any program designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorised access to a computer system. It encompasses a broad family of threats: viruses, which attach themselves to legitimate files and spread when shared; ransomware, which encrypts your files and demands payment for their release; spyware, which secretly monitors your activity and transmits data to attackers; and trojans, which disguise themselves as legitimate software to trick you into installing them.

Malware typically enters an organisation through email attachments, downloads from untrusted websites, infected USB drives, or vulnerabilities in unpatched software. Once inside a network, it can spread rapidly and cause significant damage. The best protection includes keeping software updated, never opening unexpected attachments, and ensuring your device's antivirus protection is active and current.

---

**Tab 3: Social Engineering**

Social engineering is the art of manipulating people into revealing confidential information or taking actions that compromise security. Unlike hacking, which exploits technical weaknesses, social engineering exploits human weaknesses — trust, helpfulness, fear, curiosity, and the desire to be polite.

Common techniques include pretexting, where an attacker creates a fabricated scenario (such as posing as an IT support technician) to extract information; baiting, where an infected USB drive is left in a public area hoping someone will plug it in; and vishing (voice phishing), where attackers call employees and impersonate authority figures to request sensitive data.

Defending against social engineering requires awareness and a healthy scepticism. Always verify the identity of anyone requesting sensitive information, never feel pressured to bypass security procedures, and report anything that feels unusual to your security team immediately.

---

**Tab 4: Insider Threats**

Not all cybersecurity threats originate externally. Insider threats involve employees, contractors, or business partners who — intentionally or accidentally — compromise organisational security. Malicious insiders may steal data for financial gain, sabotage systems out of grievance, or sell access to external attackers.

Accidental insiders are far more common and cause harm through simple mistakes: sending a file to the wrong email address, falling for a phishing attack, mishandling sensitive data, or failing to follow security policies. Organisations should implement the principle of least privilege — ensuring people only have access to the data and systems they need for their specific role. Every employee with access to company data carries a responsibility to handle it with care.

### PAGE 4 — Cybersecurity FAQ
**Template:** Accordion
**Purpose:** Answer the most common questions employees have about cybersecurity using a collapsible FAQ format.

**Section 1: Do I really need a different password for every account?**

Yes — and this is one of the most important habits you can develop. If you reuse the same password across multiple accounts and one is breached, attackers will automatically test your credentials on other sites — a technique called credential stuffing. A breach of one low-importance account (such as a shopping website) can quickly cascade into a breach of your email, work systems, or banking.

Use a password manager to generate and store unique, complex passwords for every account. You only need to remember one strong master password. Your IT team can recommend approved tools for your organisation.

---

**Section 2: Is it safe to use public Wi-Fi for work?**

Public Wi-Fi networks — in cafés, hotels, airports, and conference centres — are inherently insecure. Attackers on the same network can potentially intercept your traffic and capture login credentials in a "man-in-the-middle" attack. If you must use public Wi-Fi, always connect through your organisation's Virtual Private Network (VPN), which encrypts your traffic and shields it from other users.

Avoid accessing sensitive systems or confidential documents on public Wi-Fi without VPN protection. When in doubt, use your mobile phone's hotspot instead — it is significantly more secure than an open public network.

---

**Section 3: What should I do if I accidentally click a suspicious link?**

Stay calm and act quickly. Do not enter any information on any webpage that opened. Disconnect your device from the internet immediately — this can prevent malware from communicating with external servers. Then contact your IT or security team straight away and be completely honest about what happened: the message you received, the link you clicked, and what happened next.

Do not attempt to investigate or fix the problem yourself. Your security team has specialist tools to assess and contain the threat. Reporting quickly is the most important thing you can do — incidents caught early are far less damaging than those discovered days later.

---

**Section 4: How do I know if an email is genuine?**

Check the sender's full email address, not just the display name — attackers often use addresses like "support@microsoft-helpdesk.net" that look plausible but are not official domains. Look for urgency or pressure, requests for sensitive information, and generic greetings ("Dear Customer" rather than your name).

Hover over links before clicking to see the actual URL — does it match the organisation it claims to represent? Be especially cautious of unexpected attachments. If in doubt, contact the supposed sender through a known, trusted channel (not by replying to the suspicious email) to verify whether they sent it.

---

**Section 5: What is multi-factor authentication and why does it matter?**

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) requires you to verify your identity in two or more ways before gaining access to a system. Typically this combines something you know (your password) with something you have (a one-time code sent to your phone or generated by an authenticator app).

Even if an attacker steals your password through phishing or a data breach, they cannot access your account without the second factor. MFA is one of the single most effective security controls available. Enable it on every account that supports it — especially email, cloud storage, financial systems, and any platform storing sensitive data.

### PAGE 5 — The Anatomy of a Phishing Email
**Template:** Text with Media
**Purpose:** Visually break down the elements of a phishing email so learners can recognise them in the real world.

**Section title:** How to Spot a Phishing Email

**Body text:**

Phishing emails have become increasingly sophisticated, but they almost always contain identifiable warning signs if you know what to look for. Understanding the anatomy of a phishing email — the elements attackers manipulate to deceive you — is one of the most practical skills you can develop.

**The sender address** is often the first clue. Attackers register domains that closely resemble legitimate ones: "paypa1.com" instead of "paypal.com", or "support@microsoft-helpcentre.org" instead of a genuine Microsoft domain. Always check the full email address, not just the display name in your inbox.

**The subject line** is typically designed to trigger an emotional response — urgency ("Immediate action required"), fear ("Your account has been compromised"), or curiosity ("You have a pending payment"). These emotional triggers are intentional: the attacker wants you to react before your critical thinking engages.

**The body of the email** often contains subtle errors — slightly awkward phrasing, unexpected capitalisation, or inconsistencies in formatting. Legitimate companies invest in professional communications. Imperfections are a red flag.

**The call to action** always involves clicking a link or opening an attachment. Before clicking any link, hover your cursor over it to reveal the actual URL. If it does not match the organisation it claims to represent, do not click.

Finally, legitimate organisations will never ask you to confirm your password or full bank details by email. If any email asks for this, it is fraudulent — regardless of how convincing it looks.

When in doubt: delete it, and report it.

### PAGE 6 — Inside a Corporate Network
**Template:** Image Hotspots
**Purpose:** Help learners understand which parts of their workplace environment carry cybersecurity risk, and why.

**Image description:**
A clean flat-design or isometric illustration of a typical office environment showing: a reception desk with a visitor sign-in screen, employee workstations in an open-plan area, a server room with a locked door, a ceiling-mounted Wi-Fi access point, a networked printer/scanner in a shared space, and an internet gateway/firewall at the network perimeter. Style: clean corporate illustration with labelled zones.

**Hotspot 1**
Label: Internet Gateway / Firewall
Approximate position: top-right
Popup: The firewall acts as a barrier between your internal network and the public internet, filtering traffic based on security rules and blocking known threats. However, it cannot stop threats that are invited in — such as malware downloaded by an employee clicking a malicious link. It is one layer of defence, not the only one.

**Hotspot 2**
Label: Employee Workstation
Approximate position: centre-left
Popup: Each employee's device is a potential entry point for attackers. Workstations should always run up-to-date operating systems and antivirus software. Employees should lock their screens when stepping away, avoid installing unapproved software, and never leave sensitive information visible in shared workspaces.

**Hotspot 3**
Label: Server Room
Approximate position: top-left
Popup: Servers store the organisation's most critical data — customer records, financial information, intellectual property. Physical access must be strictly controlled. Only authorised personnel should enter, and access logs should be reviewed regularly. A physical breach can be as damaging as a digital one.

**Hotspot 4**
Label: Wi-Fi Access Point
Approximate position: centre-right
Popup: Corporate Wi-Fi should use strong encryption and require authenticated access. Guest networks must be completely separated from the internal corporate network. A poorly configured access point can allow attackers within physical range to intercept traffic or access internal systems without ever touching a keyboard.

**Hotspot 5**
Label: Shared Printer / Scanner
Approximate position: bottom-centre
Popup: Networked printers and scanners are frequently overlooked as security risks. They store copies of recently printed documents internally and can be compromised like any other networked device. Employees should collect sensitive printouts immediately and use PIN-release printing wherever available.

**Hotspot 6**
Label: Visitor Sign-In Area
Approximate position: bottom-left
Popup: Visitor management systems can be exploited by social engineers posing as delivery personnel, contractors, or auditors to gain physical access. Always verify visitor identities, never hold secure doors open for unknown individuals (known as tailgating), and report anyone in a restricted area without visible identification.

### PAGE 7 — Cybersecurity Myths Busted
**Template:** Click & Reveal
**Purpose:** Learners click on a common belief to reveal why it is wrong — a memorable way to challenge assumptions.

**Card 1**
Front: "I'm not important enough to be a target."
Back: Every employee is a target. Attackers often focus on less senior employees precisely because they may have fewer personal security precautions in place, while still having access to systems, credentials, and data. A compromised junior account can serve as a stepping stone to reach more sensitive targets within the organisation.

**Card 2**
Front: "Our IT team will catch anything dangerous."
Back: Technology is only one layer of defence. No security system is 100% effective, and sophisticated attacks are specifically designed to bypass automated defences. Your IT team sets up protections, but only you can control whether you click a suspicious link, share your password, or plug in an unknown USB drive.

**Card 3**
Front: "Strong passwords are impossible to remember."
Back: A passphrase — three or four random words strung together, such as "purple-lamp-ocean-fork" — is both highly secure and far easier to remember than a complex string of characters. Better still, a password manager generates and remembers unique passwords for every account, so you only need to recall one strong master password.

**Card 4**
Front: "HTTPS means a website is safe."
Back: HTTPS means the connection between your browser and the website is encrypted — it does not mean the website itself is trustworthy. Phishing websites routinely use HTTPS and display the padlock icon. Always verify that the domain name is correct and that you arrived at the site through a trusted route, not a link in an email.

**Card 5**
Front: "I'd know if my device were infected."
Back: Modern malware, particularly spyware and certain ransomware strains, is specifically designed to operate silently for weeks or months — collecting credentials, monitoring keystrokes, or waiting for an activation command. Your device may look and behave entirely normally while a serious breach is under way.

**Card 6**
Front: "Cybersecurity is too technical for non-IT people."
Back: The most impactful security behaviours require no technical expertise at all — verifying email senders, using unique passwords, locking your screen, and reporting suspicious activity. Cybersecurity awareness is a mindset, not a skill set. The habits you build today genuinely make your whole organisation safer.

### PAGE 9 — Five Principles of Staying Safe Online
**Template:** Carousel
**Purpose:** Present the five core safe-behaviour principles as a navigable slide deck learners can work through at their own pace.

**Slide 1: Think Before You Click**

The most dangerous moment in cybersecurity is the split second before you click a link or open an attachment. Attackers rely on impulse — they craft messages designed to trigger an immediate reaction before your critical thinking engages. Before clicking anything, ask yourself: Was I expecting this? Do I recognise the sender? Does the link destination look right when I hover over it?

If anything feels off, stop. Verify through a separate, trusted channel before taking any action. Taking ten extra seconds to pause can prevent a breach that takes weeks to recover from. Slowing down is not paranoia — it is your most powerful security tool.

---

**Slide 2: Use Strong, Unique Passwords**

A strong password is long (at least 14 characters), random, and completely unique to each account. Avoid using names, dates, or dictionary words. Never reuse a password across accounts — if one service is breached, credential stuffing attacks will immediately test your password elsewhere.

A password manager removes the burden of memorising multiple complex credentials: it generates, stores, and autofills passwords securely, meaning you only need to remember one strong master password. This single change dramatically reduces your personal risk exposure.

---

**Slide 3: Enable Multi-Factor Authentication**

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) adds a second layer of security to your accounts. Even if an attacker obtains your password through a breach or phishing attack, they cannot access your account without the second factor — typically a time-sensitive code sent to your phone or generated by an authenticator app.

Enable MFA on every account that offers it, prioritising email, cloud storage, financial systems, and any work platform. MFA is one of the most effective individual security steps you can take, and it takes under a minute to set up on most services.

---

**Slide 4: Keep Everything Updated**

Software updates are primarily about security, not new features. When developers discover a vulnerability, they release a patch. Attackers actively scan for systems running outdated software and can exploit known vulnerabilities — sometimes within hours of a patch being published.

Enable automatic updates on your operating system, browser, and applications wherever possible. If your organisation manages updates centrally through IT, never delay or dismiss them. Keeping software current is one of the simplest and most effective things you can do to reduce your risk.

---

**Slide 5: Report, Don't Ignore**

If you notice something suspicious — an unusual email, an unexpected login notification, a file you don't recognise, a colleague behaving strangely with data — report it to your IT or security team immediately. Many breaches go undetected for months because employees didn't report early warning signs.

There is no such thing as an over-reported security concern. Your security team would far rather investigate a false alarm than discover a real incident weeks after it began. Reporting promptly is not an overreaction — it is exactly what protects your organisation.

### PAGE 14 — Data Protection: Fact or Myth?
**Template:** True / False
**Purpose:** Challenge a common misconception about who bears responsibility for data protection in an organisation.

**Statement:**
Under data protection regulations, individual employees who handle customer data are personally exempt from responsibility — only the organisation itself is legally accountable.

**Answer:** FALSE

**Explanation:**
While organisations bear primary regulatory accountability under data protection legislation such as GDPR, individual employees can face personal consequences for deliberate or seriously negligent mishandling of personal data. Employment law, professional standards, and contractual obligations all place clear responsibilities on individuals who handle personal information in their work. More fundamentally, every person who accesses customer or colleague data has an ethical duty to treat it with the same care they would want applied to their own personal information. Data protection is a shared responsibility — not something that belongs exclusively to the legal or compliance team.

### PAGE 17 — What You Have Learned
**Template:** Summary & Takeaways
**Purpose:** Recap the six most important learning points and direct learners to the final assessment.

**Title:** What You Have Learned

**Intro text:**
You have now covered the essential foundations of cybersecurity awareness. Here are the most important points to carry with you — in the workplace and beyond.

**Key Points:**

1. **Every employee is a target.** Cybercriminals do not discriminate by seniority or job role — a compromised junior account can unlock an entire organisation. *(emphasis: high)*

2. **Phishing is the most common entry point.** Always verify unexpected messages before clicking links, opening attachments, or providing any information. *(emphasis: normal)*

3. **Strong, unique passwords and MFA are your most effective personal defences.** Use a password manager and enable multi-factor authentication on every account that supports it. *(emphasis: high)*

**Question 3 — 1 point (True / False)**
Malware always causes obvious, immediate symptoms such as pop-ups or system slowdowns.

Answer: FALSE ← CORRECT

Explanation: Many malware types — particularly spyware and certain ransomware strains — are designed to operate silently for extended periods, collecting credentials or waiting for activation with no visible impact on device performance.

**Question 4 — 1 point (MCQ)**

Explanation: Legitimate IT teams never need to ask for your password — they have system-level tools to manage accounts. This is a classic vishing (voice phishing) social engineering attack designed to exploit your trust in authority.
